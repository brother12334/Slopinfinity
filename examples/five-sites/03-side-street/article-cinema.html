The last independent cinema

One screen, a hand-lettered marquee, and a small staff who keep the projector running every night.

Sample story written for this demo. The cinema, the people and the quotes are fictional.

The Halloway has one screen, two hundred and twelve seats and a marquee that still takes its letters by hand. On most evenings Dee Marsh climbs the ladder at about five, swaps out the title and checks that every bulb along the edge is working. When one goes, she replaces it the same day. "People notice," she says. "A dark bulb looks like we're closing."

They aren't, though it has been close. The multiplexes on the edge of the city took the big releases years ago, and the Halloway learned to live on what was left: second runs, old prints, a Sunday matinee for families, and a late show on Fridays that started as a joke and now sells out.

Tomas Ruiz has run the place for eleven years. He keeps the books on paper in the office behind the concession stand and can tell you, without looking, which week of the year is the slowest. "The second week of February," he says. "Nobody wants to leave the house." That is the week he books the films he likes most, because he knows the people who come are the ones who would come anyway.

"A dark bulb looks like we're closing."

The building needs work. The heating runs on a boiler older than most of the staff, and the back rows lean slightly to the left. A volunteer group meets on the first Monday of each month to paint, fix seats and argue about the program. Last spring they re-covered forty seats in a single weekend.

What keeps the Halloway open is less nostalgia than habit. Regulars have their rows. A retired teacher has come to the Tuesday early show for nine years and always sits in the same aisle seat, row F. Teenagers who once came to the family matinee now come back on dates. When the projector failed during a sold-out screening two winters ago, nobody asked for a refund; they waited forty minutes in the lobby while Dee found the part.

But the arithmetic isn't easy, and Ruiz doesn't pretend otherwise. Tickets cover the rent in a good month and not in a bad one, and the concession stand makes up the difference. He has turned down two offers for the building. "Someone would make it into flats," he says, "and the street would lose the only lit sign after nine."

On the night we visited, the marquee read the name of a film from 1962 and the queue stretched past the bakery next door. Dee was up the ladder again, fixing a letter that had slipped. Below her, someone asked whether it was the last showing. She laughed and said there would be another one tomorrow.
