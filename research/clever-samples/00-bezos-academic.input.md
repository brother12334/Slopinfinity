Accounts of Jeff Bezos tend to begin in a garage in Bellevue, Washington, where in 1994 he started an online bookstore after leaving a senior position at the hedge fund D.E. Shaw (Stone, 2013). The story is accurate as far as it goes, but it obscures more than it explains. Bezos, born in Albuquerque in 1964, had trained in electrical engineering and computer science at Princeton; the venture was a calculated bet on the early growth of the web, not a leap in the dark. Nor was retail, in the long run, the most consequential thing he built. That distinction arguably belongs to Amazon Web Services, launched in 2006 to rent out computing infrastructure the company had first developed for its own use. Within a decade, firms as large as Netflix ran on it. If Amazon is now better understood as infrastructure than as a store, the shift began there.

His other ventures complicate any single characterization. Bezos founded the aerospace firm Blue Origin in 2000 and bought The Washington Post in 2013 for $250 million; in July 2021, roughly two weeks after handing the chief executive role to Andy Jassy and becoming executive chair, he flew to the edge of space aboard Blue Origin's New Shepard capsule. Assessments of his record diverge sharply, and they tend to divide along lines of economic position. For consumers, the achievement is one of price and convenience. Journalists and labor researchers have instead documented warehouse conditions that critics regard as the cost of that convenience, and Khan (2017) argued that antitrust doctrine, with its narrow focus on consumer prices, was poorly equipped to recognize the kind of market power Amazon had accumulated. Which account deserves more weight is a question less about Bezos himself than about what we expect very large firms to be accountable for.
